## Review model

Use two passes and three reviewer lenses.

The two passes answer different questions:

1. `Fast-screen`: Can a reviewer quickly understand the candidate and find relevant work without friction?
2. `Deep-review`: Does the work provide credible evidence for the target role, discipline, and level?

The three lenses represent different hiring concerns:

1. `Recruiter`: eligibility, positioning, relevance, access, and clear signals.
2. `Hiring manager`: scope, ownership, judgment, collaboration, complexity, and outcomes.
3. `Craft reviewer`: discipline-specific quality and professional maturity.

Do not claim these lenses predict a particular company's decision. Use them to expose different interpretations of the same evidence.

## Evidence integrity

Maintain four layers:

1. `Observation`: What is visibly or textually present.
2. `Interpretation`: What the evidence may indicate.
3. `Risk or opportunity`: Why the interpretation matters for the target job.
4. `Recommendation`: What the candidate could consider changing.

Label an interpretation when it is not explicit. Never elevate an inference into a fact.

Use precise locations:

- Website URL and section heading
- Project and subsection
- PDF page
- Screenshot number and visible region
- Navigation path
- Link or asset name

If exact location is unavailable, describe the closest identifiable context.

## Evidence grades

### Strong evidence

Use when the portfolio provides specific, relevant, and credible evidence for the JD requirement. Strong evidence usually connects context, candidate action, rationale, and outcome. Not every requirement needs metrics.

### Partial evidence

Use when the portfolio mentions or suggests the capability but lacks one or more important elements such as ownership, depth, rationale, constraint, validation, or outcome.

### Missing evidence

Use when a material JD requirement is not demonstrated in the inspected portfolio. This does not mean the candidate lacks the skill; it means the portfolio does not currently prove it.

### Contradictory evidence

Use sparingly when visible evidence conflicts with the requirement or with another important claim. Explain the conflict. Do not use this grade merely because execution is weak.

### Not assessable

Use when access, confidentiality, missing material, or tool limitations prevent a reasonable judgment.

## JD requirement importance

Classify without numerical weights:

- `Essential`: A core responsibility, explicit requirement, or repeated outcome central to the role.
- `Emphasized`: A meaningful differentiator or recurring expectation.
- `Supporting`: Useful context, tool familiarity, or secondary qualification.

Explain ambiguous classifications. Treat boilerplate requirements cautiously.

Translate abstract JD language into portfolio evidence. Example:

```text
JD language:
"Navigate ambiguous and complex problems."

Observable evidence:
- Explains what was unknown or contested
- Identifies business, user, technical, legal, or organizational constraints
- Compares plausible directions
- Shows how evidence changed the approach
- Explains a consequential decision and tradeoff
```

Do not rely on keyword frequency or ATS-style matching.

## Fast-screen pass

Review the landing experience and the first visible project signals.

Check:

- Role and specialty clarity
- Target level plausibility
- Value proposition without inflated claims
- Project relevance and curation
- Project title and thumbnail informativeness
- Candidate ownership visibility
- Shipped, concept, school, volunteer, or speculative status
- Navigation and access friction
- Contact path
- Basic polish and trust

Return:

- `Likely first takeaway`
- `Signals that help`
- `Early-screen risks`
- `What a reviewer may misunderstand`

Avoid universal time claims. The purpose is constrained-attention simulation, not a stopwatch test.

## Deep-review dimensions

Apply only dimensions relevant to the target discipline.

### Positioning and curation

- Does the portfolio communicate a coherent professional identity?
- Do selected projects demonstrate the JD's most important capabilities?
- Is breadth useful, or does it dilute the target?
- Are the strongest and most relevant projects easy to find?
- Does the portfolio distinguish real, speculative, school, and confidential work?

### Problem and context

- Is the user, business, organizational, or technical problem clear?
- Is there evidence that the problem existed?
- Are goals and intended outcomes explicit?
- Are domain and product context sufficient for an unfamiliar reviewer?
- Is scope understandable?

### Ownership and collaboration

- Does the candidate distinguish `I` from `we`?
- Are team composition and responsibilities clear?
- Does the candidate show their decisions rather than list team activity?
- Are influence, facilitation, conflict, and alignment demonstrated when relevant?
- Is leadership calibrated to career stage?

### Judgment, process, and rigor

- Are methods and activities connected to questions and constraints?
- Is rationale shown, not merely a list of standard process steps?
- Are rejected options and tradeoffs visible?
- Does the work show iteration based on evidence?
- Is the process authentic rather than formulaic?
- Does the case explain deviations from an idealized process?

Do not require a named design framework or a linear double-diamond story.

### Craft and execution

- Does execution meet the target discipline's quality bar?
- Are artifacts legible enough to support claims?
- Are interaction, content, visual, research, or engineering details explained?
- Are accessibility and inclusion considered where relevant?
- Do final artifacts connect back to the stated problem and constraints?

### Outcomes and impact

- Does the case explain what changed?
- Is success linked to the original goal?
- Are quantitative, qualitative, operational, strategic, or learning outcomes credible?
- Is the candidate transparent when outcomes are unavailable?
- Does the portfolio distinguish output, outcome, and impact?

Never demand invented numbers. Accept credible alternatives:

- Usability findings
- Customer or stakeholder feedback
- Adoption or behavior signals
- Reduced operational burden
- A decision enabled or risk avoided
- Shipped scope and subsequent iteration
- What was learned when a project stopped

### Reflection and growth

- Does the candidate explain surprises, failure, adaptation, or changed judgment?
- Is learning specific enough to affect future practice?
- Across projects, is there evidence of increasing scope or maturity?
- Does reflection avoid performative or generic statements?

### Story and engagement

- Can the case stand alone without a live voiceover?
- Is an executive summary available for long cases?
- Does hierarchy support scanning and deeper reading?
- Is there enough context without burying decisions?
- Do visuals carry explanatory value rather than decoration?

### Portfolio experience

- Is navigation predictable?
- Can the reviewer return to the project list?
- Is important content usable on likely viewport sizes?
- Are typography, spacing, contrast, and image treatment coherent?
- Are loading states, broken assets, or intrusive effects undermining the work?
- Does the medium suit the target role?

## Three reviewer lenses

### Recruiter lens

Prioritize:

- Clear target role and relevant history
- Obvious access to representative work
- Alignment with baseline JD requirements
- Credible presentation
- Contact and application readiness

Do not pretend to make legal eligibility decisions or infer protected characteristics.

### Hiring manager lens

Prioritize:

- Level-appropriate scope and complexity
- Personal ownership
- Decision quality
- Cross-functional work
- Outcomes, learning, and ability to explain tradeoffs
- Evidence that transfers to the hiring team's problems

### Craft lens

Load the selected role lens. Judge professional reasoning and execution, not visual polish alone.

## Artifact-specific checks

### Website

When tools permit, inspect:

- Home, work index, representative cases, about, and contact
- Desktop and mobile behavior
- Working navigation and back links
- Password flow
- Broken links and failed assets
- Loading and large media
- Semantic headings, keyboard basics, alt text, contrast, and reduced-motion concerns
- Whether animation or custom code strengthens or obstructs comprehension

Do not overstate automated accessibility or performance checks.

### PDF or slide deck

Inspect:

- Page count and pacing
- Page numbers and orientation
- Text density and minimum readable size
- Image resolution
- Whether it works as a standalone document or live presentation
- Project transitions
- Links and contact details
- Confidentiality markings

Do not judge a presentation deck as though it were necessarily intended for silent reading; state the assumed use.

### Screenshots or partial samples

Audit only visible material. Do not infer full navigation, responsiveness, performance, or omitted project content.

## Complete-portfolio synthesis

Treat the portfolio as a curated system, not a bag of independent projects.

Create an inventory:

| Item | Type | Status | Candidate role | Capabilities shown | JD relevance | Contribution to overall positioning | Access status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Inspect every submitted or reachable case study when practical. Use the inventory to determine:

- Whether the collection tells a coherent professional story
- Whether multiple projects prove a capability or merely repeat the same process
- Whether the most relevant projects are easy to discover
- Whether important JD capabilities are absent across the entire collection
- Whether an outdated, speculative, school, or unrelated project weakens positioning
- Whether a specialized candidate is being presented as an undifferentiated generalist
- Whether project order creates the right first impression
- Whether claims, role descriptions, dates, or outcomes contradict one another
- Whether breadth demonstrates range or dilutes depth

Give two levels of findings:

1. `Portfolio-wide`: patterns that recur across cases or concern curation, ordering, positioning, and combined capability coverage.
2. `Case-specific`: issues unique to one project or artifact.

Do not paste the same portfolio-wide recommendation under each case. Reference its finding ID and explain only the case-specific variation.

If an item cannot be opened, keep it in the inventory as `Not assessable`. Do not exclude it silently.

## Evidence matrix

Use columns:

| JD requirement | Importance | Expected evidence | Evidence found | Location | Grade | Interpretation and hiring risk | Recommended response |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Keep JD requirements meaningfully distinct. Combine true duplicates, but do not hide missing requirements inside a broad category.

## Recommendation design

Every material recommendation should include:

1. `Finding`
2. `Evidence location`
3. `Why it matters for this JD`
4. `Suggested change`
5. `Concrete example or structure`
6. `Candidate facts needed`

Use priorities:

- `Must address`
- `High-value improvement`
- `Quick win`
- `Optional refinement`
- `Do not prioritize for this JD`

Examples may show:

- A proposed section order
- A before/after information structure
- A sentence pattern with placeholders
- The questions a missing section should answer
- Which projects to lead with, keep, condense, or consider removing

Do not:

- Modify files
- Present fabricated final copy
- Recommend inserting a metric merely to look senior
- Force every project into the same template
- Turn all work into a heroic individual narrative

## Seniority calibration

### Student, new graduate, or career changer

Look for:

- Foundations and learning ability
- Clear problem solving
- Honest project status
- Transferable experience
- Thoughtful constraints, even in school or volunteer work
- Ability to critique and improve one's own work

Do not require organizational impact or production access they could not reasonably have.

### Early career

Look for:

- Reliable execution
- Contribution clarity
- Use of feedback
- Collaboration
- Developing judgment
- Evidence of learning across projects

### Mid-level

Look for:

- Independent project ownership
- Tradeoffs and prioritization
- Cross-functional collaboration
- Iteration under real constraints
- Outcomes beyond deliverable completion

### Senior

Look for:

- Framing ambiguous problems
- Handling complexity and multiple constraints
- Influence and alignment
- Strategic and execution depth
- Measurable or otherwise credible impact
- Raising quality beyond individual screens or tasks

### Lead, Staff, or Principal

Look for:

- Multi-team or organizational scope
- Direction setting
- Systems, standards, or capability building
- Influence without relying on authority
- Mentorship when relevant
- Long-horizon outcomes and tradeoffs

Do not infer seniority solely from years of experience, famous employers, polished visuals, or job titles.

## Fairness and anti-bias

- Judge job-related evidence, not personality similarity or cultural fit.
- Avoid penalizing candidates for nontraditional education or career paths.
- Do not infer age, ethnicity, gender, disability, family status, nationality, or other protected traits.
- Account for NDA and access constraints without treating absence as proof of inability.
- Distinguish English fluency or copy polish from design judgment unless language craft is job-critical.
- Treat AI-polished language cautiously; prefer specific decisions and artifacts over confident prose.
- Flag formulaic case-study structure without assuming bootcamp work is inferior.
- Recognize credible qualitative outcomes when business metrics are unavailable.

## Re-audit

Create a comparison table:

| Prior finding | Status | New evidence | What improved | Remaining gap | Next action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Use statuses:

- `Resolved`
- `Partially resolved`
- `Unresolved`
- `Not assessable`

Do not praise wording changes that still lack underlying evidence.
